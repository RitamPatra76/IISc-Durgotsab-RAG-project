## 3. Daily Schedule & Rituals (Utsab)
### Mahalaya (মহালয়া)
- **Date**: 10 October 2026 Saturday
- **Overview**: The fortnight of the ancestors ends and the fortnight of the goddess begins
- **Rituals & Timings**:
  - **04:00**: Mahishasuramardini on air (মহিষাসুরমর্দিনী) — *Birendra Krishna Bhadra's recitation, as it has opened the season for close to a century.*
  - **Morning**: Tarpan for the ancestors (তর্পণ)
  - **Day**: Chokkhu Daan, the giving of eyes (চক্ষুদান)

### Maha Shashthi (মহাষষ্ঠী)
- **Date**: 16 October 2026 Friday
- **Overview**: Bodhon, when the goddess is woken out of season
- **Rituals & Timings**:
  - **by 8:30 AM**: Bodhon, Astradan, Amantran and Adhibas (বোধন, অস্ত্রদান, আমন্ত্রণ ও অধিবাস) — *At the TMC ground.*
  - **6:30 PM**: Puja inauguration and Sandhya Arati (পুজো উদ্বোধন, সন্ধ্যারতি)

### Maha Saptami (মহাসপ্তমী)
- **Date**: 17 and 18 October 2026 Saturday and Sunday
- **Overview**: Nabapatrika is installed on one day and worshipped on the next
- **Rituals & Timings**:
  - **17 Oct, 7:00 AM**: Nabapatrika Snan and Prabesh (নবপত্রিকা স্নান ও প্রবেশ)
  - **18 Oct, by 5:53 AM**: Saptami Puja (সপ্তমী পূজা)
  - **6:30 PM**: Sandhya Arati and cultural evening (সন্ধ্যারতি, সাংস্কৃতিক অনুষ্ঠান)

### Maha Ashtami (মহাষ্টমী)
- **Date**: 19 October 2026 Monday

  - **7:26 to 8:14 AM**: Sandhi Puja (সন্ধি পুজো) — *The last twenty-four minutes of Ashtami and the first twenty-four of Nabami.*
  - **6:30 PM**: Sandhya Arati and cultural evening (সন্ধ্যারতি, সাংস্কৃতিক অনুষ্ঠান)

- **প্রধান ফটক** [Marker 175, Grid G3]: Main Gate Marker 175 on the campus map, grid G3. The gate every auto and cab driver knows, off C. V. Raman Road at Prof. C. N. R. Rao Circle. Under four hundred metres to the ground.

- **মত্তিকেরে রোড গেট** [Marker 177, Grid F2]: Mattikere Road Gate Marker 177, grid F2. The Yeshwantpur side, closest to the new hostel blocks. Straight east along the road past the hostels.
- **কেন্দ্রীয় বিদ্যালয় গেট** [Marker 174, Grid F2]: KV Gate Marker 174, grid F2, beside Kendriya Vidyalaya. Useful if you are dropping children at the school and walking across afterwards.
- **এনআইএএস গেট** [Marker 178, Grid A2]: NIAS Gate Marker 178, grid A2, at the National Institute of Advanced Studies. Rarely used by visitors and a genuinely long walk, but the prettiest way in.

### Getting to Campus / Public Transport
- **Summary**: Ask for the Tata Institute. Nobody driving in this city calls it anything else; every auto driver in Malleswaram and Yeshwantpur knows the gate.
- **Yeshwantpur Railway Station**: Under 2 km away, closest rail option. Prepaid auto from platform 6 exit.
- **Bengaluru City Railway Station**: About 7 km. Prepaid taxis run from platform 1.
- **Airport**: Kempegowda International Airport ~35 km. BMTC shuttle runs every 15 minutes.
- **Bus Stops**: Prof. C.N.R. Rao Circle, yellow overbridge, and Yeshwantpur tollgate.
- **Accessibility**: Everything is at ground level and flat; the pandal is reachable with a wheelchair or pram.

## 10. Traditional Bengal Art Forms (Shilpa)
### Dhak, Dhol and Kansar (ঢাকের বাদ্যি) — 1606
Indian Institute of Science Bengaluru

### Pratima Shilpa (Idol Making) (প্রতিমা শিল্প) — Sculpture
The idol-maker's year begins on Rath Yatra, when the previous year's bamboo frame is worshipped and reused. At many old family pujas the same kathamo has carried the goddess for over a century, the skeleton persists, only the skin is remade.

### Krishnanagar Clay Dolls (কৃষ্ণনগরের মাটির পুতুল) — Sculpture
The Ghurni cooperative applied for a Geographical Indication in 2021 and the Registry approved it as the Krishnagar Clay Doll in July 2026, after decades in which the name was borrowed by mass-produced imitations. Over 300 people in Ghurni work in clay.

### Daker Saj (ডাকের সাজ) — Craft
Daker saj is one of the few Indian craft names that is an accidental fossil of global trade, it records a supply chain running from a German foil mill to a Calcutta post office to a potter's lane.

### Sholar Saj (Sholapith) (শোলার সাজ) — Craft
The same craft dresses both ends of a Bengali life, the shola topor and mukut worn by a bride and groom at a wedding, and the white ornament that goes into the river with the goddess.

### Chalchitra (Painted Backdrop) (চালচিত্র) — Painting
The chalchitra has escaped the idol. Theme pujas now hire patuas to paint chalchitra logic across an entire pandal, walls, ceiling and corridor, so visitors walk inside the backdrop instead of looking at it.

### Patachitra and Pater Gaan (পটচিত্র ও পটের গান) — Painting
The patuas of Naya are Muslim by faith and bear the title Chitrakar, and they paint and sing Hindu mythology for a living. Bengal Patachitra received its Geographical Indication on 28 March 2018.

### Alpona (Ritual Floor Art) (আলপনা) — Painting
Alpona is not rangoli. Rangoli is dry, coloured powder on a grid of dots; alpona is wet, white and freehand. In 2019 a 1.4 km alpona along Lake Road in south Kolkata was billed as the world's longest street-art installation.

## 15. Historical Facts & Timeline (90 milestones)
- **The Chandi is a chapter, not a book** [c. 400-600 CE]: The Devi Mahatmya is not an independent scripture. It is chapters 81 to 93 of the Markandeya Purana, inserted into a far older text somewhere between 400 and 600 CE. Wendy Doniger dates the insertion to about 550 CE and the surrounding Purana to about 250 CE.
- **Carved proof from a Rajasthan wall** [608 CE]: The oldest hard evidence for the Devi Mahatmya is not a manuscript but a wall. The Dadhimati Mata temple inscription in Rajasthan, dated 608 CE, quotes the text directly, so it was already scriptural within a century of composition.
- **The first worshippers were two ruined men**: In the Devi Mahatmya's frame story the first Durga Puja is performed by a deposed king, Suratha, and a merchant, Samadhi, cheated by his own wife and sons. At the sage Medhas's hermitage they make a clay image on a riverbank and worship it.
- **Spring, not autumn, is canonical**: Scripture places Suratha's worship in Chaitra, which makes Basanti Puja the timely one and the autumn festival the untimely exception. A ritual trace survives, in Basanti Puja no ghat is installed on Shashthi, because no awakening is needed.
- **The oldest Durga killed nobody**: The word Durga appears in six Rigvedic hymns and a deity Durgi in the Taittiriya Aranyaka, but none of these is the buffalo-slayer. The name is far older than the goddess we picture.
- **Arjuna hymns Durga before battle**: In Bhishma Parva chapter 23 of the Mahabharata, Krishna tells Arjuna to invoke Durga before the war; Arjuna steps down from his chariot and praises her. Most scholars read this hymn, like Yudhishthira's parallel prayer in the Virata Parva, as a late interpolation roughly contemporary with the Puranic goddess tradition rather than older than it.
- **The oldest Mahishasuramardini is Rajasthani** [1st c. BCE - 1st c. CE]: The earliest known images of Durga slaying the buffalo are terracotta plaques from Nagar in Rajasthan, dated to the 1st century BCE to 1st century CE, evidence of goddess worship there before the Kushans.
- **A twelve-armed Durga dated by inscription** [401 CE]: Cave 6 at Udayagiri near Vidisha holds a multi-armed Durga treading on the buffalo's head, beside an inscription of Chandragupta II dated Gupta year 82, equal to 401 CE, among the earliest securely datable images of the theme in India.
- **Mahalaya is for the dead, not the goddess**: Mahalaya is the new moon that ends Pitri Paksha and begins Devi Paksha. Its central act is Tarpan, offering water with black sesame and kusha grass to the ancestors, standing in a river. The belief that Durga Puja starts on Mahalaya is a modern conflation.
- **Bengal's stone goddesses came first** [8th-12th c.]: Pala and Sena Bengal produced Mahishasuramardini images in stone and brass in quantity, concentrated in the old districts of Dhaka, Faridpur, Tipperah and Bakarganj, where they served as family and village deities long before any zamindar's festival.
- **The blood chapter that forbids itself** [c. 10th-11th c.]: The Kalika Purana's Rudhiradhyaya, its 'blood chapter', is the rare Puranic text to discuss human sacrifice, and it hedges the practice about with conditions until almost nothing is left of it. It may be done only with a prince's consent, and only before a war or in imminent danger. Anyone physically disabled, anyone related to a Brahmin, and anyone unwilling to die is unfit for it.
- **Rama's autumn puja is not in Valmiki** [15th c.]: The story of Rama waking Durga out of season to defeat Ravana, the Akalbodhan on which all of Bengal's autumn festival rests, does not appear in Valmiki's Ramayana. It reaches Bengal through the Kalika Purana and, decisively, through Krittibas Ojha.
- **The hundred and eighth lotus** [15th c.]: In the Bengali telling, Rama must offer 108 blue lotuses; one vanishes, and Rama, called Padmalochana, lotus-eyed, reaches to offer one of his own eyes instead. The goddess stops his hand. This scene is Bengali, not Sanskrit.
- **A rulebook before the first zamindar** [c. 1375-1575]: Three Sanskrit digests codified the Bengali rite long before any landlord's spectacle, Shulapani's Durgotsavaviveka, Vidyapati's Durgabhaktitarangini, and Raghunandana Bhattacharya's Durgapujatattva, which effectively standardised practice.
- **Bengal's oldest claim is a clay goddess** [997 CE (traditional)]: The Mrinmoyee temple at Bishnupur claims a Durga Puja running since 997 CE, founded by the Malla king Jagat Malla. The 2023 festival was billed as its 1,027th, though the date is dynastic tradition, not epigraphy.
- **The most-repeated 'first' puja** [c. 1580]: Raja Kangshanarayan of Taherpur, in what is now Bangladesh, is the figure most often credited with the first grand autumn Durga Puja, reportedly spending over eight lakh rupees. Dates in the sources range from 1480 to 1606, and historians reject the claim that he invented the rite.
- **The Nadia Raj began with a puja** [1606]: Bhabananda Majumdar founded the Nadia Raj in 1606 under Jahangir and began a celebrated Durga Puja at his capital, Matiyari. His descendant Raja Krishnachandra Roy, who ruled 1728-1782, moved the seat to Krishnanagar and expanded the puja into one of Bengal's grandest.
- **Kolkata's oldest puja is 1610** [1610]: Lakshmikanta Gangopadhyay Majumdar and his wife Bhagawati Devi began the Sabarna Roy Choudhury Durga Puja at the Barisha Atchala in 1610. It has run continuously ever since and is now spread across eight locations.
- **A different name for every age**: The Kumari is named by her age, and the sixteen names are fixed, Sandhya at one, Saraswati at two, on through Aparajita at ten and Bhairavi at twelve, to Annada or Ambika at sixteen.
- **Durga has no daughters in any Purana** [c. 16th c.]: The family tableau, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartikeya and Ganesha flanking Durga, is a Bengali folk invention of roughly the sixteenth century. The sons appear in the Puranas; the daughters do not. That is why the children look uninterested in the battle.
- **Bengal turned a war goddess into a daughter**: The terrifying Puranic Chandika underwent what one historian calls a plebeianisation in Bengal, transformed into a commoner's daughter who visits her parents once a year, and received parental tenderness rather than fear.
- **The festival everyone else fasts for**: Navaratri elsewhere in India is observed with fasting. Bengal vigorously rejected that, Durga Puja is the week of the best fish and meat a household can manage.
- **Kola Bou is not Ganesha's wife**: The banana-plant bride beside Ganesha is the Nabapatrika, nine plants bound with white aparajita and a yellow thread, each embodying a form of the goddess. She is Durga's vegetal body, not anyone's wife.
- **Nine plants, nine goddesses**: The Nabapatrika's nine plants each carry a goddess, banana is Brahmani, colocasia Kalika, turmeric Durga, jayanti Kartiki, bel Shiva, pomegranate Raktadantika, ashoka Shokarahita, arum Chamunda, and paddy Lakshmi.
- **Why she stands beside Ganesha**: One folk explanation ties the Nabapatrika's placement beside Ganesha to his epithet Ashtadashaushadhisrishti, creator of eighteen medicinal plants, the herbal goddess beside the lord of herbs. It is not attested in the standard puja manuals, which tell other stories. What is certain is that she is not his bride.
- **Exactly forty-eight minutes**: Sandhi Puja occupies the last 24 minutes of Ashtami and the first 24 of Nabami, exactly 48 minutes, performed on clock time rather than daylight, with 108 lamps lit and 108 lotuses offered.
- **The crack between two tithis**: Sandhi Puja commemorates the moment Durga, ambushed by Chanda and Munda against the rules of battle, manifested Chamunda from her third eye. The rite is potent precisely because it belongs to neither tithi.
- **The real immersion is a reflection**: On Dashami a vessel of water is set before the goddess and a mirror angled so her reflection falls in it, and the reflection is immersed. Priests hold that this, not the river, is the moment the divinity leaves the clay.
- **The goddess's ride forecasts the year**: The Bengali panjika reads the goddess's arrival and departure vehicle as an omen, elephant for peace and plenty, boat for flood and a good harvest both, horse for war and unrest, palanquin for epidemic. Newspapers still print the forecast every autumn.
- **A girls' rite with no priest**: In Punyipukur Vrata, girls of five to nine dig a miniature pond in the courtyard and tend it daily for a month from Chaitra to Baisakh, so the village pond will not run dry. It is an unscriptural women's vow, no mantra and no priest are required.
- **Dhaka is named after a goddess** [12th c. (traditional)]: By tradition Ballal Sen of the Sena dynasty dreamt of a buried image of the goddess, recovered it and enshrined her as Dhakeshwari, 'Goddess of Dhaka'. The city is widely held to take its name from her; the temple is today Bangladesh's only state-owned National Temple.
- **Dhak, dhol and kanshi are three things**: The dhak is the large barrel drum slung from the shoulder and struck with two sticks on one head only; the dhol is smaller and played on both heads, stick in the right hand and palm in the left; the kanshi is a brass plate. In Puja the dhak leads and the others follow.
- **No guest may leave unsweetened**: Bijoya extends the festival by up to a fortnight, juniors touch elders' feet, men exchange the triple embrace of kolakuli, and no visitor may leave without mishti-mukh. To send a guest away unsweetened is inauspicious.
- **The 15th-century text we read is 19th-century** [early 1800s]: Krittibas lived c. 1381-1461, but the Krittivasi Ramayan everyone quotes is the recension edited by Jaygopal Tarkalankar and printed at the Serampore Mission Press in the early nineteenth century. Manuscript versions differ substantially.
- **The family that leased away Calcutta** [1698]: The Sabarna Roy Choudhurys, whose 1610 puja is widely regarded as Kolkata's oldest, are the family from whom the East India Company obtained the lease of Sutanuti, Gobindapur and Kalikata, the three villages that became the city.
- **The puja that followed Plassey** [1757]: Nabakrishna Deb, later Robert Clive's Persian munshi, began building Shobhabazar Rajbari in 1757 and started its Durga Puja the same year, weeks after the Battle of Plassey. It set the template for the Calcutta babu's puja.
- **Clive probably never came** [1757]: The famous tale that Robert Clive gave thanks to Durga at Shobhabazar after Plassey is very likely invented. Deb became Clive's munshi only after 1757, and the story rests on a single anonymous painting.
- **A nine-gun salute for Kali** [c. 1800]: Governor-General Lord Wellesley ordered a nine-gun salute in honour of Kali, and British officials were entertained at Bengali mansions with nautch and alcohol. Conspicuous consumption, not bhakti, was the central motif.
- **Seven thousand goddesses a year** [1839]: In 1839 alone, the Friend of India reported roughly seven thousand Durga images made in Calcutta, a scale of ritual manufacture that already anticipated the modern festival.
- **A festival listed in the newspaper** [1814]: The Calcutta Gazette of 20 October 1814 records, 'The Hindu holidays of the Doorga Poojah began yesterday and will continue until the 25th instant.' The festival was already a fixture of the colonial calendar.
- **The ban that was a newspaper column** [1840]: Everyone repeats that in 1840 the Company forbade its servants from attending the babu Pujas. No such order has been produced. Rachel Fell McDermott's full study of the Bengal goddess festivals records no ban; what it records for 1840 is an attack on European attendance in the Bengal Hurkaru of 7 October, and Europeans still attending as late as 1855. Official unease is real and datable elsewhere, the 1833 despatch, the Lords debate on idolatry in August 1840, the March 1841 order withdrawing troops and gun salutes from Hindu festivals. A press campaign has been promoted into a prohibition.
- **The first people's puja was not to Durga** [1790 (some sources 1761)]: Twelve men of Guptipara in Hooghly, shut out of a household Puja, raised a subscription and held their own, and baro plus yaar gave the word baroyari. Two things are usually left out. Rachel Fell McDermott writes that the first recorded baroyari was performed to Jagaddhatri, not Durga. And Tapati Guha-Thakurta, who tells the story, hedges it throughout as what has often been recounted, without vouching for the date. The most solidly documented case of a community taking over a Puja is not Guptipara at all but Bankura in 1793, where people of every caste applied to the East India Company for permission to collect money and hold one.
- **Calcutta's first community puja** [1909/1910]: The Bhowanipore Sanatan Dharmotsahini Sabha organised Calcutta's first baroyari Durga Puja at Balaram Bose Ghat Road. Sri Aurobindo published his Durga Stotra in his Bengali journal Dharma on the occasion.
- **A puja born of bankruptcy** [1919]: Baghbazar Sarbojanin exists because a family ran out of money. In October 1919 the Sarkars of 55 Baghbazar Street could no longer afford their private puja, so the locality took it over as the Nebubagan Baroyari. It was renamed Baghbazar Sarbojanin in 1926.
- **The Mother as nation** [1882]: Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay wrote Bande Mataram in the 1870s and published it inside Anandamath in 1882. Its Mother draws directly on Durga and Kali; Congress first sang it in 1896, and it became the anthem of the 1905 Swadeshi movement after Bengal was partitioned.
- **A gymnasium's puja for revolutionaries** [1926]: The Simla Byayam Samity, a gymnasium of the kind that routinely fronted for revolutionary organisation, began its Durga Puja in 1926 under Atindra Nath Bose, with Vivekananda's brother Mahendranath Dutta. It drew Subhas and Sarat Bose and Bhupendranath Dutta.
- **Bose kept the pandal open to everyone** [1930s]: As president of the Simla Byayam Samity's Durga Puja committee, Subhas Chandra Bose carried on the practice the Samity had begun at its founding in 1926, of opening the pandal to women and the general public. He presided over the Kumartuli Sarbojanin committee in 1938.
- **The Tagores gave up Durga Puja** [19th c.]: Durga Puja was abolished at Jorasanko Thakurbari when Debendranath Tagore embraced the image-rejecting Brahmo Samaj. He replaced it with the Magh Utsav, in which no image appears. Rabindranath grew up in a house without a pratima.
- **A monk holds an image worship** [1901]: Swami Vivekananda instituted Durga Puja at Belur Math in 1901 with Sarada Devi's permission, remarkable because Hindu sannyasins do not normally perform image worship. He worshipped nine girls as Kumaris himself.
- **Durga's lion used to be a horse** [until the 1880s]: Bengali sculptors had never seen a lion, so early zamindari images gave the goddess a creature that looks more like a horse or a donkey. Only after real lions reached the Calcutta zoo in the 1880s did local clay sculptors learn to make a lion that looked like one.
- **Ornaments that arrived in the mail** [19th c.]: Daker Saj is named after the postal service. The silver foil and sequins were imported, mainly from Germany, and arrived by post, dak in Bengali. Hence 'the dress that came by post'.
- **One arch, ten Mahavidyas** [c. 300 years]: The classical Bengali image is the ekchala, the whole family under a single arch, against a painted chalchitra backdrop that the patua painters called pata lekha, often carrying the ten Mahavidyas. The craft has nearly vanished in this century.
- **A fire broke the ekchala** [1937-38]: A fire destroyed the idols at Kumartuli Sarbojanin the year Subhas Chandra Bose was asked to preside. Gopeshwar Pal, the self-taught 'lightning sculptor' later honoured in Europe, rebuilt the whole group in about 24 hours, on five separate platforms. That committee's ekchala tradition ended that night.
- **Nobody knows how old Sindoor Khela is**: Two traditions compete, that Sindoor Khela began about two hundred years ago in zamindar households to build bonhomie among housewives, or that it is as old as the Bengali festival itself, some four hundred years. Neither is documented.
- **The potters were imported** [c. 1757]: Kumartuli's clay modellers came from Krishnanagar in Nadia, itself a colony of Dhaka migrants, brought to Calcutta around 1757 to make Nabakrishna Deb's Durga, and permitted to settle in the quarter the Company had zoned for potters.
- **The frame is worshipped before the goddess**: The bamboo-and-wood armature of the idol, the kathamo, is itself worshipped months before the festival, traditionally on Rath Yatra or Ulto Rath. In many old houses the same kathamo is used year after year.
- **Two coats of clay, each with a name**: The idol takes two named clay coats, ek mete, sticky entel mati from the Ganga bed mixed with rice husk, sun-dried three or four days; then du mete, fine sandy bele mati, dried five or six. Cracks between them are bridged with strips of cloth.
- **Never fired, made to dissolve**: Durga idols are sun-dried, never baked, properly terracruda, not terracotta. The clay is engineered to come apart in water, which is exactly what immersion asks of it.
- **The eyes are given last**: Chokkhu Daan, the eye-giving, is the final act of idol-making and a ritual rather than a finish. It is performed on Mahalaya, and is understood to be the moment life enters the clay.
- **The soil nobody can date**: Clay for the idol is said to require punya mati begged from a courtesan's doorway. The custom is real and still practised in places, but it has no textual source and no traceable origin, and sex workers' groups note that the soil is revered for a week while the women are denied everything else.
- **A railway doctor started Delhi's puja** [1910]: The Delhi Durga Puja Samiti was founded at Kashmiri Gate in 1910, largely through the efforts of a railway doctor, Hemchandra Sen, beginning as a baroyari at the Roshanpura Kali Mandir near Nai Sarak.
- **No rice at the Shobhabazar puja**: At Shobhabazar Rajbari no grain is offered, not to the goddess, not to visitors. Only monda-mithai, pantua, peraki, radhaballabhi, motichur laddu, cooked on the premises by Brahmin cooks.
- **A doll of milk instead of blood**: At the Sabarna Roy Choudhury puja in Barisha, rice and potato are excluded from the goddess's bhog, and the sacrifice offered before her is a khirer putul, a doll made of thickened milk.
- **No caste test for the Kumari**: Banglapedia records that no distinction of race, religion or caste is made in choosing the girl for Kumari Puja, and that the philosophical basis of the rite is to establish the value of women.
- **The war that changed the goddess's dress** [1939-45]: The Second World War cut off the German silver-foil imports that made Daker Saj. Sholapith, the white spongewood of Bengal's wetlands, worked by the Malakar caste, rose as its substitute and never left.
- **The broadcast that made Mahalaya** [1931]: Mahishasuramardini was first broadcast from Calcutta radio in 1931, script by Bani Kumar, music by Pankaj Kumar Mullick, Chandi recitation by Birendra Krishna Bhadra. More than any scripture, this programme fixed Mahalaya in the Bengali year.
- **The year Bengal stoned the radio station** [1976]: On 23 September 1976 All India Radio replaced Bhadra with a new programme, Devim Durgatiharinim, narrated by Uttam Kumar. AIR offices were stoned, switchboards flooded; the broadcaster apologised and restored Bhadra. Uttam Kumar is said to have called it converting a place of worship into a drawing room.
- **Most sacrifice today is a gourd**: Under Vaishnava influence, Bengali bali is now usually vegetal, banana, ash gourd, sugarcane, and mashbhakta of rice, banana, cloves and ghee. Belur Math performs the banana, ash gourd and sugarcane bali.
- **Women broke into Kumartuli in the 1990s** [1990s]: Idol-making was closed to women within living memory. Mala Pal and China Pal entered the trade in the 1990s against considerable resistance and now export their work internationally.
- **Three prizes in the first year** [1985]: The Asian Paints Sharad Shamman, inaugurated in October 1985, is the oldest and still the most coveted Puja award. Only three prizes were given in its first year; Adi Ballygunge was among the winners.
- **A Santhal sari for the goddess** [1990]: A decade before 'theme puja' had a name, the sculptor-anthropologist Meera Mukherjee made the Durga for Bakul Bagan Sarbojanin in 1990, rendering her in an Adivasi idiom and draping her in a Santhal sari.
- **Running lights invented on a plank** [1965]: Sridhar Das offered to illuminate Narua's Jagaddhatri Puja in 1965 and built the first running lights on a flat wooden board. Kashinath Neogy brought LEDs to Chandannagar in 2005; today's masters were all trained by Das.
- **London's first Durga came by donation** [1963]: In autumn 1963 Bengali students held London's first Durga Puja at the Mary Ward Centre, Russell Square. The editor Tushar Kanti Ghosh, then in London, donated the pratima. The puja moved to Camden's Hampstead Town Hall in 1966 and has stayed.
- **America's first puja was at Columbia** [1970]: The oldest community Durga Puja in the United States was held at Columbia University in 1970, organised by the East Coast Durga Puja Association, still the oldest Bengali association in North America. People drove hundreds of miles for it.
- **Delhi's Bengali quarter was a refugee colony** [1960s-1977]: Chittaranjan Park began in the early 1960s as EPDP Colony, East Pakistan Displaced Persons Colony, and was renamed for Chittaranjan Das in the 1980s. Its first Durga Puja was in 1970; the Kali Mandir held its first in 1977.
- **Bangalore's puja began with a pot** [1949]: Durga Puja in Bangalore is said to have begun around 1949 with a simple ghot puja in Malleswaram, the goddess represented by a pot of consecrated water rather than an image, organised by Bengalis from the Indian Institute of Science and the new public-sector industries. The city's first Sarbojanin puja followed in 1950.
- **A worship hall used as an ammunition dump** [1971]: During the 1971 Liberation War the Pakistan Army took over Dhakeshwari's main worship hall and used it as an ammunition store. The temple was damaged again in communal attacks between 1989 and 1992.
- **Hired off a railway platform**: Dhakis come from Murshidabad, Nadia, Bankura, Bardhaman, Malda, Hooghly, Purulia and Midnapore, converge on Sealdah station in the days before the Puja, and are hired off the platform. For the other eleven months many farm or pull rickshaws.
- **The year Puja came 35 days late** [2020]: In 2020 Ashwin held two new moons, making it a mala mas in which no auspicious rite may be performed. Durga Puja slipped into Kartik and Mahashashthi fell 35 days after Mahalaya instead of six. The last such years were 2001 and 1982.
- **Three hundred goddesses fly out a year** [2024-25]: Kumartuli exported over 240 idols in 2024 and more than 300 the following season, to Russia, the USA, Australia, Japan, Canada, Germany, Italy, France, New Zealand, Uganda and Dubai, almost all in fibreglass, because clay cannot survive air freight.
- **When the pandal became an artwork** [c. 2001]: Around the turn of the millennium, art-school graduates turned the pandal into a composite installation, structure, idol, decor, colour, lighting and music designed as one work. Bosepukur Sitala Mandir is most often credited with launching the wave.
- **2.58 per cent of a state's economy** [2019-21]: The British Council study, led by Queen Mary University of London with IIT Kharagpur, valued the creative economy around Durga Puja in West Bengal at ₹32,377 crore, equal to 2.58 per cent of the state's GDP. It was among the most rigorous studies of its kind in India, building on earlier work such as a 2013 ASSOCHAM report.
- **Decision 16.COM 8.b.15** [2021]: 'Durga Puja in Kolkata' was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity at the sixteenth session of the Intergovernmental Committee, held 13-18 December 2021, under decision 16.COM 8.b.15, nomination file 00703.
- **The title was fought over** [2019]: The Ministry of Culture wanted the element pitched as a nationwide phenomenon, 'Durga Puja of India'. The historian Tapati Guha-Thakurta, who prepared the dossier, argued that Kolkata was where the festival's longest continuous history and community character could actually be shown, and won. Hence the inscribed title, 'Durga Puja in Kolkata'.
- **The 88-foot Durga that had to be shut** [2015]: Deshapriya Park installed an 88-foot Durga in 2015, billed as the world's tallest. About 5.5 lakh people converged, a near-stampede injured eleven, and Kolkata Police closed the ground and turned the crowds back.
- **Thirty-four thousand empty pandals** [2020]: In 2020 the Calcutta High Court declared all roughly 34,000 pandals in West Bengal no-entry zones for visitors, with barricades five metres out for small pandals and ten for large. It later relented to let the dhakis inside.
- **Thirty-three thousand mandaps in Bangladesh** [2025]: Durga Puja in Bangladesh is observed at over 33,000 mandaps and temples nationwide, 33,355 in the most recent count, up from 31,461 the year before. Dhaka city alone had 252 mandaps in 2024, rising to 259 in 2025.
- **The community that mourns during Puja**: The Asur people of Jharkhand, upwards of thirty thousand in the 2011 Census, practising an animist tradition of nature and ancestor worship and not considering themselves Hindu, regard Mahishasura as their ancestor. Many stay indoors through Durga Puja to mourn him.
- **Four women ran an entire Durga Puja** [2021]: After its veteran male priest died, the 66 Pally committee in Kolkata engaged four women of the collective Shubhamastu, Nandini Bhowmick, Ruma Roy, Semanti Banerjee and Poulomi Chakrabarti, to conduct all the 2021 rituals. The theme, 'the Mother invoked by mothers'.
- **Women dhakis are barely sixteen years old** [2010]: Gokul Chandra Das of Maslandapur trained Bengal's first troupe of women dhakis, six women and four men, who played their first Durga Puja in 2010. His group now includes about thirty women.
